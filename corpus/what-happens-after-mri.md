# What usually happens after an MRI

After an MRI scan of the brain, the path forward depends entirely on what
the radiologist sees and what your clinician decides to do with that
information. There is no single fixed sequence — but several common
patterns are useful to understand so the next appointment feels less
unpredictable.

## A typical sequence

1. **The radiologist reads the scan** and writes a structured report. This
   usually takes hours to a few days.
2. **Your ordering clinician reviews the report** and contacts you with
   the result, often by phone, message, or at a follow-up appointment.
3. **If something needs more investigation**, you may be referred to a
   specialist — typically a neurologist, neurosurgeon, or
   neuro-oncologist depending on what was seen.
4. **Further tests** may be ordered: a contrast-enhanced MRI if the first
   was without contrast, an MR spectroscopy or perfusion MRI to look at
   tissue characteristics, or a CT scan for different information.
5. **A multidisciplinary team meeting** ("tumor board" or "MDT") may
   review the case before recommendations are finalized.

## Words you may see in the report

- **"Lesion"** — a general word for an area that looks different from the
  surrounding tissue. It does not mean cancer.
- **"Mass"** — an area of abnormal tissue. Also does not by itself mean
  cancer; many masses are benign.
- **"Enhancing" or "non-enhancing"** — describes how the area takes up
  contrast dye. Useful information, but not diagnostic alone.
- **"Differential"** — a list of possibilities the radiologist is
  considering, in rough order of likelihood.

A radiology report is not a diagnosis. It is a careful description of
what an imaging specialist sees, written for the clinician who will
combine it with everything else they know about you.

## What this page does not tell you

- Your specific timeline. It depends on the system, the urgency, and the
  finding.
- What a specific term in your report means for you. Ask the clinician.
