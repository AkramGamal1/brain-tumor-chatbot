# Glioma grades and what they mean

When a glioma is diagnosed, one of the first things the pathology team
determines is its **grade**. Grade is not the same as the type of glioma
(astrocytoma, oligodendroglioma, etc.) and not the same as molecular
markers — it is one piece of a larger picture used to plan care.

## What grade describes

Grade is a measure of how the tumor's cells look under a microscope and
how the tissue is behaving. Lower-grade gliomas tend to grow more slowly
and look more like normal cells; higher-grade gliomas tend to grow more
quickly and look more abnormal. The current World Health Organization
classification (updated in 2021) blends grade with molecular features
because the combination predicts behavior better than either alone.

## The grades, in plain terms

- **Grade 1**: usually well-circumscribed, slow-growing, often more
  amenable to surgical removal alone. More common in children.
- **Grade 2**: still slow-growing relative to higher grades, but more
  diffuse. Often watched closely or treated, depending on size,
  location, symptoms, and molecular features.
- **Grade 3**: more aggressive cells. Usually treated with a combination
  of surgery, radiation, and systemic therapy.
- **Grade 4**: the most aggressive — glioblastoma is the best-known
  example. Treated with a combination approach guided by current
  evidence and the patient's situation.

## Why molecular markers matter alongside grade

For some gliomas, molecular features such as **IDH mutation**,
**1p/19q co-deletion**, and **MGMT methylation** change both the
predicted behavior and the available treatments. Two tumors with the same
grade but different molecular features can have very different plans.
This is why neuro-pathology and molecular testing are routine.

## What this page is not

- Not a prediction for any individual person's outcome — those depend on
  many factors beyond grade.
- Not a substitute for the conversation with your neuro-oncology team
  about *your* specific pathology.
