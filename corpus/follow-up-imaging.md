# Follow-up imaging — why and how often

For many brain conditions the first scan is not the last. Follow-up
imaging — usually MRI — is how clinicians track whether something is
stable, growing, shrinking, responding to treatment, or coming back. The
schedule is decided by your team based on the diagnosis and what is being
watched.

## Why repeat scans matter

- Some tumors grow slowly enough that monitoring is itself a reasonable
  approach for a time, with active treatment held in reserve.
- After surgery, scans confirm what was removed and serve as a baseline
  for future comparisons.
- During or after treatment, scans show whether therapy is working.
- Long after treatment, surveillance scans look for recurrence early,
  when more options are usually available.

## A typical pattern (varies widely)

- A **post-operative MRI** within hours to a few days after surgery.
- An **early follow-up MRI** several weeks to a few months later, to
  establish a stable baseline.
- **Surveillance MRIs** at increasing intervals — every few months at
  first, then every six months, then yearly — depending on the diagnosis
  and how things look. Some people stay on regular scanning indefinitely.

The exact spacing should be a conversation between you and the team. It
is reasonable to ask: *"Why this interval? What would change it? When
would we space them further apart?"*

## Things that complicate interpretation

- **Pseudoprogression**: a temporary appearance of "growth" after
  radiation that turns out not to be true tumor growth. Repeat imaging
  often clarifies it.
- **Treatment-related changes**: scar tissue, swelling, or contrast
  patterns that look different from the original tumor and need expert
  reading.
- **Subtle changes** that need the same radiologist (or the same set of
  comparison scans) to track confidently.
