# What a biopsy is and when one is considered

Imaging shows what something *looks like*. A **biopsy** — examining a small
tissue sample under a microscope — shows what something *is*. For brain
tumors, a biopsy is often the only way to confirm what type of tumor is
present, what grade it is, and what molecular features it carries. Those
details drive the rest of the plan.

## Two main approaches for the brain

- **Stereotactic (needle) biopsy.** A small hole is made in the skull and
  a thin needle is guided to the tumor using imaging and a frame or
  navigation system. Used when removing the tumor entirely is risky or
  when only a sample is needed.
- **Open biopsy or resection.** A craniotomy — a section of the skull is
  temporarily opened — to take a larger sample, or to remove as much of
  the tumor as is safe in the same operation. The pathologist's findings
  on the removed tissue then guide further treatment.

## Why a biopsy may *not* be the immediate next step

- Some tumors have a very characteristic appearance on imaging and
  monitoring may be appropriate before any procedure.
- Some locations are too risky to biopsy safely; doctors may choose to
  treat based on imaging alone after careful discussion.
- Other tests may be ordered first to clarify the picture.

## What the pathology report typically tells the team

- The tumor type (e.g., glioma, meningioma, etc.).
- The grade — how the cells look and how aggressively they appear to be
  growing.
- Molecular markers (for example IDH, MGMT, 1p/19q for some gliomas) that
  influence treatment options and outlook.

## What this page does not tell you

- Whether *you* need a biopsy. That is a discussion between you, a
  neurosurgeon, and the rest of the team.
- The risks of any specific procedure for your situation.
