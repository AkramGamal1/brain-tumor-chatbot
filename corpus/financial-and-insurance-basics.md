# Financial and insurance basics

The financial side of a serious medical situation is its own job — often
on top of everything else. The specifics depend heavily on where you
live and what coverage you have, but a few categories of help exist
almost everywhere.

## Inside the hospital or treatment center

- **Oncology social workers** are usually the single most useful first
  call. They know the local resources, what your hospital offers, and
  how to escalate when something is denied.
- **Patient navigators / financial counselors** can walk through what is
  covered and what is not, before bills arrive.
- **Charity care or financial-assistance programs** are required at many
  US non-profit hospitals. They are often under-publicized; ask
  explicitly about them.

## Working with insurance

- **Pre-authorization** for imaging, biopsy, surgery, or radiation is
  often required. The treatment team typically handles it, but follow up
  to confirm it was approved before scheduled procedures.
- **Coverage denials** can usually be appealed. Hospital financial
  counselors are experienced with this; do not assume the first answer
  is final.
- **Out-of-network surprises** are most common with anesthesia, pathology,
  and radiology. Asking ahead about which specialists in a planned
  procedure are in-network can prevent some of them.
- **Keep a folder** — physical or digital — with bills, EOBs (explanation
  of benefits), and notes about who you spoke with. Phone calls are
  worth more if you can reference them later.

## Outside the system

- **Patient advocacy organizations** sometimes have direct grants for
  travel, lodging, or co-pays.
- **Drug-manufacturer patient-assistance programs** can help with the
  cost of specific medications.
- **Employer benefits**, including disability insurance, employee
  assistance programs, and FMLA in the US.

## What this page is not

- Not financial advice for your specific situation.
- Not a guarantee any program is currently active or covers your case.
