# Telling family and friends

Sharing a difficult diagnosis is a separate task from receiving it, and
not always an easier one. There is no script and no obligation to do it
in any particular order or at any particular pace. You get to decide who
to tell, when, how, and how much.

## Some patterns that help

- **Start with one or two people you most trust.** They can be the first
  ring of support and can help you carry the news to others when you
  are ready.
- **Decide what you want from each conversation** before having it. Some
  conversations are for emotional support. Some are practical (logistics,
  childcare, rides). Some are informational. They do not all need to be
  the same.
- **Keep a short version and a longer version ready.** Sometimes a
  one-line update is what fits. Other times a longer conversation is
  right.
- **It is OK to delegate.** A close friend or family member can update
  others on your behalf. You do not have to be the one repeating the
  news fifteen times.

## Talking with children

- Use simple, honest language. Children sense uncertainty and worry more
  if information is hidden than if it is shared at their level.
- Avoid words that mean different things to a child than to an adult
  (e.g. "tumor" or "cancer" — define what they are).
- Reassure them about the parts that will stay the same. Routines matter.
- Many cancer centers have **child-life specialists** who help families
  with these conversations.

## When other people say the wrong thing

People who care about you will sometimes say something unhelpful — out of
fear, awkwardness, or just inexperience. It is not necessarily a sign
they don't love you. You can:

- Tell them, gently, what you actually need.
- Limit time with people whose response is consistently draining.
- Ask a friend to help you set boundaries with the harder relationships.
